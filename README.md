A Three-Stage Optimization Framework for Urban Waterborne Parcel Logistics in Bergen

## Abstract

As urban parcel demand rises, trucks face growing limits in dense city centres. However, in Bergen, the same geography that constrains road delivery also creates another delivery channel: water. This thesis examines how a waterway parcel network could be designed for Bergen, with ports acting as parcel-locker locations.

The network is designed through a three-stage optimization framework. First, a partial set covering model selects port locations. Second, a symmetric capacitated boat routing model builds delivery routes under capacity and shift limits. Third, a scheduling model assigns the routes to boats and crews.

Using household data, parcel-demand data, OpenStreetMap data, and manually measured maritime-distance data, the thesis compares two port layouts and tests low, average, and peak demand days across three boat capacities and three crew sizes. Arna, Fana, and Åsane are excluded after the walking-time analysis, since the candidate ports could not cover them within a realistic walking time. The results therefore refer to the remaining central and coastal districts of Bergen.

The results show that 20-28 ports give similar walking outcomes, with average walking times around 20 minutes. Operations, however, are mainly driven by demand. The network requires 1-10 boats and 10-76 employees depending on the demand scenario, with peak days forcing the system to scale significantly. A 1500-parcel boat offers the best capacity compromise, while larger crews are most useful during peak periods. The thesis shows that waterborne parcel logistics in Bergen could be feasible in principle.
